Group assignment: travelling tracks

Some tracks live on one playlist. Others travel: the same track turns up on five different playlists. A curation team wants to know whether the ones that travel are recognisably different. Three questions, answered as one notebook, plus a short video of your group talking through what you decided.

What counts as travelling

A travelling track is one that appears on more than a certain number of playlists. We have not fixed that number for you. Two playlists? Three? Five?

Choosing where to draw that line is part of the assignment, and so is showing what happens to your answer when you move it. There is no number we are waiting for you to guess.

The three questions

  • What is one row, and how far does each track travel? Say what one row of the raw file is, and what one row of the table you build is. Then count how many playlists each track appears on. There is more than one way to count that, so show the check that convinced you yours counts playlists.
  • Add the family label. The second file sorts the 24 subgenres into six broader families, so that you can ask whether a pattern holds inside each one. Merge it onto your table: write down the row count you expect first, merge with validate= and indicator=, and deal with whatever the lookup throws at you.
  • Do the travelling tracks look different? Compare them against the tracks that appear on one playlist only, on at least two audio features and on track_popularity, with counts next to every mean. Show what three or more cut-offs do to the answer. Check whether the pattern holds inside each family. One chart, and 250 to 400 words on what you found and what it cannot tell anyone.

The data

Two files. The template reads both straight from a URL, so there is nothing to download and nothing to attach to your submission.

  • spotify_songs.csv — the same 2020 Spotify snapshot as session 02.
  • subgenre_families.csv — 24 subgenres mapped to 6 families. We wrote this one for the assignment. It is not an official Spotify taxonomy, it carries no authority, and nobody checked it before handing it to you.

Treat the second file the way you would treat any lookup table that arrives from another team: check its keys before you trust the join.

What we will notice

It is not graded, and we do read them. We will pick up on whether the row meaning is shown rather than asserted, whether you predicted the row count before merging, whether counts appear next to means, and whether the language stays on the right side of cause and effect. Chart styling, notebook length and whether your answer matches ours are not of interest.
